\begin{abstract}
The long-only minimum variance portfolio is routinely solved by assembling a sample covariance
matrix and dispatching it to a general-purpose quadratic program. Both steps are unnecessary
and expensive.

Conjugate gradients applied matrix-free to the returns matrix $X$ solve the problem in $O(nT)$
work per iteration without ever forming the $n\times n$ covariance; a primal-dual active-set
loop enforces non-negativity with negligible overhead. The covariance matrix is not a primitive
of the problem---it is a computational detour.

Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage, already standard practice for statistical reasons, turns out to be
identical to spectral preconditioning of the linear system: the same ridge term that shrinks
sample eigenvalues toward their mean also compresses the condition number and reduces CG iteration
counts dramatically. Statistical regularization and numerical preconditioning are the same
operation applied to the same matrix, a connection that does not appear to have been made
explicit in the existing literature.

Numerical experiments on S\&P~500 equity data and synthetic problems of growing size show
order-of-magnitude speedups over interior-point solvers. For standard portfolio sizes a direct
KKT solve already captures most of the gain; the matrix-free advantage of CG becomes decisive
at larger $n$, where avoiding explicit covariance assembly reduces per-step cost from $O(n^2)$
to $O(Tn)$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The long-only minimum variance portfolio allocates capital across $n$ assets to minimize portfolio return variance subject to two constraints: the weights must sum to one (the portfolio is fully invested) and must be non-negative. The
only input required in theory is the $n \times n$
covariance matrix $\Sigma$ of the $n \times 1$
vector of asset returns. In practice, $\Sigma$ is unknown and must be estimated from historical data. To this end, one uses a $T\times n$ matrix of demeaned returns $X$, where $T$ denotes the length of the estimation window (or the sample size). The classical estimator of $\Sigma$ is the sample covariance matrix $\hat \Sigma = X^\top X/T$.

This paper makes two contributions.  First, we apply conjugate gradients (CG) to $\hat \Sigma v = \mathbf{1}$
\emph{matrix free}: $\hat \Sigma = X^\top X$  never needs to be assembled explicitly.  Each CG iteration requires only two matrix-vector products
with $X$, reducing per-iteration cost from $O(n^2)$ to $O(nT)$ and eliminating $O(n^2)$ storage.  Non-negative weights are
enforced by a primal-dual active-set loop that calls the CG solver on successively smaller subproblems.

Second, we show that Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage~\cite{ledoit2004}, a method suggest to come up with a more accurate estimator of the true covariance matrix $\Sigma$ compared to the sample covariance matrix,
plays a dual role in this framework.  Statistically, it
reduces estimation error by shrinking sample eigenvalues toward their mean.  Numerically, the same modification
compresses the eigenvalue spread of the system matrix and directly accelerates CG convergence. For example, on S\&P~500 equity data, using LW shrinkage instead of the sample covariance matrix reduces the number of CG iterations from 744 to 82.
Statistical regularization and spectral preconditioning are, in this
problem, the same operation applied to the same matrix---a connection that does not appear to have been made explicit in
the existing literature.

CG of Hestenes and Stiefel \cite{hestenes1952} is a cornerstone of large-scale scientific computing; e.g., see Golub and van Loan \cite{golub2013}. However, to the best of our knowledge it has not been
applied yet
to portfolio optimisation in the matrix-free form described here. The closest related work applies GMRES to the minimum
variance problem when $\hat \Sigma$ is singular~\cite{bajeux2012}; that formulation still assembles $X^\top X$ and does not
exploit the SPD structure or consider preconditioning. General-purpose solvers such as
Clarabel~\cite{clarabel2024} handle the long-only constraint natively
but require as input an estimate of the covariance matrix.

empirical results on both synthetic and S\&P~500 data.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}
The long-only mean-variance portfolio solves
\[
\min_{w}\; w^\top\Sigma\, w - \rho\,\mu^\top w
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\mathbf{1}^\top w = 1,\quad w\geq 0~,
\]
where $\Sigma$ is the $n\times n$ covariance matrix of returns, $\mu$ is the $n\times 1$ vector of expected returns, and $\rho\geq 0$ controls the risk-return trade-off; setting $\rho=0$ recovers the pure long-only minimum variance portfolio.
Since both $\Sigma$ and
$\mu$ are unknown, in practice they must be replaced by estimates. Let $X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times n}$ denote the demeaned return matrix for $n$ assets over the previous $T$ trading periods. The feasible problem formulation then is
\[
\min_{w}\; w^\top\hat\Sigma\, w - \rho\,\hat\mu^\top w
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\mathbf{1}^\top w = 1,\quad w\geq 0~.
\]
The classical choice for estimating $\Sigma$
the sample covariance matrix $\hat\Sigma = X^\top X/T$, for which $w^\top\hat\Sigma\, w = \norm{Xw}^2/T$, and the problem becomes
\[
\min_{w}\;\frac{\norm{Xw}^2}{T} - \rho\,\hat\mu^\top w
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\mathbf{1}^\top w = 1,\quad w\geq 0~.
\]
This reframing is key: the variance term is now a normalised squared norm of $Xw$, and the gradient $w\mapsto X^\top(Xw)$ can be evaluated with two matrix--vector products, without ever forming $X^\top X$.

\paragraph{Dual problem}
Including multipliers for both constraints, the full Lagrangian is
\[
\mathcal{L}(w,\, \lambda,\, \nu)
= \frac{\lVert Xw\rVert^2}{T} - \rho\hat\mu^\top w
  - \lambda(\mathbf{1}^\top w - 1) - \nu^\top w\;,
\]
where $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ is the budget multiplier and $\nu\geq\mathbf{0}$ is the vector of
non-negativity multipliers. The stationarity condition
\[
\tfrac{2}{T}\, X^\top X w = \lambda\mathbf{1}+\nu+\rho\hat\mu
\]
gives $w^* = \tfrac{T}{2}(X^\top X)^{-1}s$ with $s = \lambda\mathbf{1}+\nu+\rho\hat\mu$.
Substituting into $\mathcal{L}$:
\[
\mathcal{L}(w^*,\,\lambda,\,\nu)
= \tfrac{1}{T}(w^*)^\top X^\top X w^* - s^\top w^* + \lambda\;.
\]
From stationarity $\tfrac{1}{T}X^\top X w^* = \tfrac{s}{2}$, so the first term equals
$\tfrac{1}{2}s^\top w^*$, giving
\[
g(\lambda,\,\nu)
= \tfrac{1}{2}s^\top w^* - s^\top w^* + \lambda
= -\tfrac{1}{2}s^\top w^* + \lambda\;.
\]
Substituting $w^* = \tfrac{T}{2}(X^\top X)^{-1}s$ yields the dual function
\[
g(\lambda,\,\nu)
= \inf_w\; \mathcal{L}(w,\,\lambda,\,\nu)
= \lambda - \tfrac{T}{4}\,
  (\lambda\mathbf{1}+\nu+\rho\hat\mu)^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}
  (\lambda\mathbf{1}+\nu+\rho\hat\mu)\;,
\]
defined when $X$ has full column rank; the dual problem is
$\max_{\lambda,\,\nu\geq\mathbf{0}}\; g(\lambda,\,\nu)$.
The feasible set $\{w:\mathbf{1}^\top w=1,\,w\geq\mathbf{0}\}$ is compact and
$w=\tfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}$ is strictly feasible, so Slater's condition holds and
strong duality obtains.

At the optimum, complementary slackness ($\nu_i w_i = 0$ for all $i$) partitions assets
into two types. \emph{Active} assets ($w_i>0$) have $\nu_i=0$; stationarity then gives
\[
\tfrac{2}{T}\,(X^\top Xw)_i = \lambda+\rho\hat\mu_i\;,
\]
equating each active asset's marginal variance to the budget shadow price adjusted for its
expected return. \emph{Excluded} assets ($w_i=0$) satisfy
\[
\nu_i = \tfrac{2}{T}\,(X^\top Xw)_i - \lambda - \rho\hat\mu_i \geq 0~.
\]
Their marginal variance exceeds this threshold, so holding them at a positive weight would
increase variance net of return. The primal--dual loop in Section~\ref{sec:primaldual} is a direct
implementation of these conditions: it terminates precisely when primal and dual feasibility
both hold, which---together with stationarity from each inner solve---is sufficient for
global optimality.

\section{Positive-Definite Reduction}\label{sec:pd}

Ignoring the inequality constraints for now, the Lagrangian for the equality-constrained problem is:
\[
\mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = \frac{\|Xw\|^2}{T} - \lambda(\mathbf{1}^\top w - 1)~ + \rho \hat \mu~.
\]
Setting the derivative to zero gives the stationarity condition
$\frac{2}{T}\hat\Sigma\, w = \lambda \mathbf{1} + \rho\hat \mu$, where $\hat\Sigma = X^\top X/T$ (or $\hat \Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}$ with shrinkage).
For $\rho = 0$, this reduces to $\frac{2}{T}\hat\Sigma\, w = \lambda\mathbf{1}$, determining the solution up to a scalar.

\paragraph{Positive-definite reduction}
When $\rho = 0$ (pure long-only minimum variance), stationarity gives $2\hat\Sigma\, w = \lambda\mathbf{1}$,
so $w$ is a scalar multiple of $\hat\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}$.
Setting $v_1 = \hat\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}$ and writing $w = c\,v_1$, the budget constraint
$\mathbf{1}^\top w = 1$ \tcb{scales}
\[
  c = \frac{1}{\mathbf{1}^\top v_1} \quad \mbox{and} \quad w = \frac{v_1}{\mathbf{1}^\top v_1}~.
\]
When $\rho \neq 0$, stationarity gives $2\hat\Sigma\, w = \lambda\mathbf{1} + \rho\hat \mu$, so
$w = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,\hat\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\,\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat \mu$.
Setting $v_1 = \hat\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}$ and $v_2 = \hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat \mu$ (two SPD solves), the budget
constraint determines the multiplier
\[
  \lambda = \frac{2 - \rho\,(\mathbf{1}^\top v_2)}{\mathbf{1}^\top v_1}~,
\]
and the weights follow as $w = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\,v_1 + \tfrac{\rho}{2}\,v_2$.
In both cases the $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ saddle-point system is never needed and $\lambda$ is
recovered analytically rather than as part of the solve.
The long-only problem therefore reduces to solving a symmetric positive definite system
$\hat\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}\,v = b$ for one or two right-hand sides, subject to the remaining
inequality constraint $w \geq 0$; the latter is addressed in Section~\ref{sec:primaldual},
which presents two strategies for doing so.


\section{Enforcing Non-Negativity}\label{sec:primaldual}

Section~\ref{sec:pd} reduces the long-only problem to solving an SPD system, leaving
$w \geq 0$ as the sole remaining constraint.  Two strategies are available.

\paragraph{Active-set strategy.}
The first strategy separates the budget constraint from the non-negativity constraint.
The budget-only problem is solved over a dynamically adjusted \emph{active set} of
assets; non-negativity is then enforced iteratively without ever modifying the inner
solve.

In the \emph{primal step}, any asset with a negative weight is dropped from the active
set and the budget-constrained SPD system is re-solved on the reduced universe.  Once
all active weights are non-negative, the \emph{dual step} checks the KKT gradient
condition for every excluded asset: global optimality requires every excluded asset $i$
to satisfy
\[
  \nabla_i f(w) \;\geq\; \lambda\;,
  \qquad
  \nabla_i f(w) = \tfrac{2}{T}(X^\top Xw)_i - \rho\hat\mu_i\;,
\]
where $\lambda$ is the budget Lagrange multiplier recovered analytically from
Section~\ref{sec:pd}.  An excluded asset that violates this condition would decrease
portfolio variance if held at a small positive weight; it is re-added to the active set
and the loop repeats.  The loop terminates when both primal and dual feasibility hold;
together with stationarity from the inner solve this certifies global optimality.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Active-set outer loop (wraps any inner solver $f$)}\label{alg:elim}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Inner solver $f$;\; tolerance $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$
\Ensure Weight vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$;\; total inner iteration count $k$
\State $\mathrm{active} \leftarrow \{1,\ldots,n\}$;\; $k \leftarrow 0$
\Loop
  \State $(w_{\mathrm{a}},\, k_{\mathrm{step}}) \leftarrow f(\mathrm{active})$;\; $k \leftarrow k + k_{\mathrm{step}}$
  \If{$\exists\, i \in \mathrm{active}$ with $w_{a,i} < -\varepsilon$}
    \State $\mathrm{active} \leftarrow \mathrm{active} \setminus \{i : w_{a,i} < -\varepsilon\}$
      \hfill\textit{(primal step: drop violators)}
  \Else
    \State $w_i \leftarrow w_{a,i}$ for $i \in \mathrm{active}$;\; $w_i \leftarrow 0$ for $i \notin \mathrm{active}$
    \State $g_i \leftarrow \tfrac{2}{T}(X^\top Xw)_i - \rho\hat\mu_i$
    \State $\lambda \leftarrow \mathrm{mean}_{j \in \mathrm{active}}(g_j)$
    \State $\mathcal{V} \leftarrow \{i \notin \mathrm{active} : g_i < \lambda - \varepsilon\}$
    \If{$\mathcal{V} = \emptyset$}
      \State \textbf{break} \hfill\textit{(KKT satisfied; globally optimal)}
    \EndIf
    \State $\mathrm{active} \leftarrow \mathrm{active} \cup \mathcal{V}$
      \hfill\textit{(dual step: re-add violators)}
  \EndIf
\EndLoop
\State \Return $w$, $k$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Simplex-projection strategy.}
The second strategy treats the two constraints as a single geometric object: the
probability simplex
$\Delta_n = \{w \in \mathbb{R}^n : \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1,\, w \geq 0\}$.
Rather than separating budget and non-negativity, gradient steps of the form
\[
  w_{k+1} = \Pi_{\Delta_n}\!\left(w_k - \tau\,\nabla f(w_k)\right)
\]
project directly onto $\Delta_n$ after each descent step, where $\Pi_{\Delta_n}$
is the Euclidean projection.
Duchi et al.\ \cite{duchi2008} show that $\Pi_{\Delta_n}$ can be computed in
$O(n\log n)$ by a single sort-and-threshold pass, making each iteration cheap.
There is no outer loop: both constraints are handled simultaneously, and the method
terminates when the step size $\|w_{k+1} - w_k\|$ falls below a tolerance.

The gradient $\nabla f(w) = \tilde{X}^\top(\tilde{X}w) - \tilde{X}^\top\mathbf{0}
= \tilde{X}^\top(\tilde{X}w)$ is evaluated matrix-free in $O(nT)$ using two
matrix-vector products with $\tilde{X}$, at the same cost per step as the CG inner
solver.  The Lipschitz constant $L = \lambda_{\max}(\tilde{X}^\top\tilde{X})$, needed
for the step size $\tau = 1/(2L)$, is estimated once by power iteration on $\tilde{X}$,
again matrix-free.

\medskip
The two strategies make different tradeoffs.  The active-set loop solves a sequence of
linear systems and benefits from fast inner solvers (Section~\ref{sec:solvers}); its
convergence to global optimality is certified by the KKT check.  The simplex-projection
approach is single-stage and loop-free, but converges in $O(\kappa)$ gradient steps
versus $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ for CG, so it is most competitive when the condition number is
moderate or the matrix-free $O(nT)$ cost dominates.
Section~\ref{sec:solvers} develops concrete implementations of both strategies.


\section{Computational Methods}\label{sec:solvers}

\subsection{CG Method}\label{ssec:cg}

The inner solver $f$ for Algorithm~\ref{alg:elim} is conjugate gradients applied to the
$n_a \times n_a$ SPD system derived in Section~\ref{sec:pd}. The action of $\hat\Sigma_a$
on a vector requires only two matrix-vector products with $\tilde{X}_a$; the matrix itself
is never formed.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{CG inner step (inner solver $f$ for Algorithm~\ref{alg:elim})}\label{alg:cg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Active-asset index set $\mathrm{active}$;\; problem data $\tilde{X}$
\Ensure Active-asset weights $w_{\mathrm{a}}$;\; CG iteration count $k$
\Function{CgSolve}{active}
\State Let $\tilde{X}_{\mathrm{a}} \leftarrow \tilde{X}_{:,\mathrm{active}}$,\;
       $n_a \leftarrow |\mathrm{active}|$
\State Build matrix-free operator $\mathcal{S}$ of size $n_a \times n_a$:
  \Statex\hspace{2em}
    $v \;\mapsto\; \tilde{X}_{\mathrm{a}}^\top(\tilde{X}_{\mathrm{a}}\,v)$
    \hfill\textit{($\hat\Sigma_a$ applied matrix-free)}
\State Solve $\mathcal{S}\,v = \mathbf{1}$ via CG
\State \Return $v\,/\,(\mathbf{1}^\top v)$,\; iteration count
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

When an asset is dropped in the primal step, $\mathcal{S}$ is rebuilt for the reduced active
set and CG restarts from scratch on the smaller system.

\qquad \kappa = \kappa(\hat\Sigma_a)\;,
\]
where $\lVert e\rVert_{\hat\Sigma_a} = (e^\top\hat\Sigma_a\, e)^{1/2}$ denotes the energy norm
and $\kappa$ is the spectral condition number of the active-asset submatrix.

\subsection*{Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury Preconditioner}
One possibility for preconditioning the system matrix $A = \omega X_a^\top X_a + \gamma I$ is to use a low-rank approximation of the matrix $X_a$ and then apply the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury \cite{golub2013} identity
$
\left(I+UCV\right)^{-1}=I-U\left(C^{-1}+VU\right)^{-1}V.
$
We then utilize the idea that $UCV$ is a low-rank approximation to a large square matrix and that $\left(C^{-1}+VU\right)$ is a small matrix. If additionally $V=U^\top$ is a matrix with orthogonal columns we get
$\left(I+UCV\right)^{-1}=I-U\left(C^{-1}+I\right)^{-1}U^\top.$
We now perform the low-rank approximation of $X_a$ using the truncated singular value decomposition. Obviously, the quality of such an approximation depends on the singular value decay of $X_a$.  We start by factoring out $\gamma$ and substitute the rank-$k$ SVD of $X_a \approx U_k S_k V_k^\top$
to get the approximation
\begin{equation}
    A \approx \underbrace{\gamma\!\left(I + V_k \underbrace{\tfrac{\omega}{\gamma}S_k^2}_{C} V_k^\top\right)}_{M}~.
\end{equation}
Since $V_k^\top V_k = I_k$, we now obtain the preconditioner
\begin{equation}
    M^{-1} = \frac{1}{\gamma}\!\left(I - V_k\,\mathrm{diag}(d_k)\,V_k^\top\right),
    \qquad
    d_i = \frac{\omega\sigma_i^2}{\omega\sigma_i^2 + \gamma}.
\end{equation}
%

Applying this matrix to a vector $v$, the preconditioner is given via
%
\begin{equation}
    M^{-1}v = \frac{1}{\gamma}\!\left(v - V_k^\top\bigl(d_k \odot (V_k v)\bigr)\right)~.
\end{equation}

\subsection{CVXPY Reference Implementation}

Before developing the Krylov solvers we establish a reference solution using CVXPY \cite{diamond2016}, a domain-specific
language for convex optimization. The minimum variance problem maps directly onto a least-squares objective, which CVXPY
dispatches to a general-purpose interior-point solver.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{CVXPY reference solver}\label{alg:cvxpy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Returns matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times n}$;\; shrinkage $\alpha$;\; return tilt $\rho\hat \mu$;
\Ensure Weight vector $w$;\; interior-point iteration count $k$
\State Declare $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$ as a CVXPY variable
\State Minimise $\tfrac{1}{T}\|Xw\|^2 - \rho\hat \mu^\top w$
  \Statex\hspace{1.5em} subject to $\mathbf{1}^\top w = 1$,\; $w \geq \mathbf{0}$
\State Dispatch to Clarabel interior-point solver \cite{clarabel2024}
\State \Return $w$,\; $k$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The formulation is transparent and correct, and CVXPY handles the long-only constraint natively. We use the Clarabel
interior-point solver \cite{clarabel2024}, which dispatches the problem as a second-order cone program. Its cost is
substantial: interior-point methods require $O(n^3)$ work per iteration, and the overhead of problem construction and
solver communication adds further latency. On the S\&P~500 problem ($n = 495$) this amounts to roughly 1.6 seconds
(Table~\ref{tab:sp500}), making CVXPY unsuitable for production use but ideal as a ground-truth reference.

All three methods in this paper (CVXPY, KKT direct, and CG) solve the same optimization problem and produce identical
portfolio weights up to numerical tolerance. The comparison is purely computational: the question is not which method
finds a better portfolio, but which finds the same portfolio fastest.

The speedup figures in Table~\ref{tab:sp500} should be read with this in mind. CVXPY and
Clarabel are general-purpose tools: the same code handles additional convex constraints---sector limits, turnover bounds, factor-exposure constraints---without modification. The CG method exploits the specific structure of the pure
long-only minimum variance problem and would require rework to accommodate constraints beyond the budget and
non-negativity requirements. The speedup therefore conflates two effects: genuine algorithmic efficiency from exploiting
problem structure, and the overhead of a framework designed for a far broader problem class. For the standard long-only
constraint the full speedup is available; practitioners requiring richer constraint sets face a narrower tradeoff and
may find a specialized QP solver a more appropriate baseline.

\subsection{Proximal Gradient}\label{ssec:proximal}

An alternative to the active-set loop is to enforce the simplex constraint directly at every
iteration via Euclidean projection.  The minimum variance objective with Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage
can be written as
\[
  \min_{w \in \Delta_n}\;\tfrac{1}{2}\|\tilde{X}w\|^2,
  \qquad
  \Delta_n = \{w \in \mathbb{R}^n : \mathbf{1}^\top w = 1,\; w \geq \mathbf{0}\},
\]
where $\tilde{X} = [\sqrt{(1-\alpha)/T}\,X;\,\sqrt{\alpha}\,R]$ as before and the probability
simplex $\Delta_n$ captures both budget and long-only constraints simultaneously.  The gradient
of the objective is $\nabla f(w) = \Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}\,w$ with
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}} = \tilde{X}^\top\tilde{X}$; the Lipschitz constant of the gradient is
$L = \lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}})$.  The projected gradient iteration is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proxgrad}
  w_{k+1} = \Pi_{\Delta_n}\!\left(w_k - \tau\,\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}\,w_k\right),
  \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{2L},
\end{equation}
where $\Pi_{\Delta_n}$ denotes Euclidean projection onto the simplex.

\paragraph{Simplex projection.}
Duchi et al.\ \cite{duchi2008} give an $O(n\log n)$ closed-form algorithm for $\Pi_{\Delta_n}$:
sort $v$ in decreasing order to obtain $\mu_1 \geq \cdots \geq \mu_n$, find the largest index
$\rho$ such that $\mu_\rho > \tfrac{1}{\rho}\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{\rho}\mu_j - 1\bigr)$, set
$\theta = \tfrac{1}{\rho}\bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{\rho}\mu_j - 1\bigr)$, and return
$[\Pi_{\Delta_n}(v)]_i = \max(v_i - \theta,\,0)$.  The projection requires a single sort and a
linear scan; it is the inner loop of Algorithm~\ref{alg:proximal}.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\caption{Proximal gradient solver}\label{alg:proximal}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Stacked return matrix $\tilde{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{(T+n_R) \times n}$;\;
         tolerance $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$;\; $k_{\max} = 1{,}000$
\Ensure Weight vector $w \in \Delta_n$
\State Assemble $\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}} \leftarrow \tilde{X}^\top\tilde{X}$
  \hfill\textit{($n\times n$, assembled once)}
\State $L \leftarrow \lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}})$;\quad $\tau \leftarrow (2L)^{-1}$
\State Initialise $w_0 \in \mathbb{R}^n$ randomly;\; $k \leftarrow 0$
\Loop
  \State $w_{k+1} \leftarrow \Pi_{\Delta_n}(w_k - \tau\,\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}\,w_k)$
    \hfill\textit{(gradient step + simplex projection)}
  \If{$\|w_{k+1} - w_k\|_2 \leq \varepsilon$ \textbf{or} $k \geq k_{\max}$}
    \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
  \State $k \leftarrow k + 1$
\EndLoop
\State \Return $w_{k+1}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\tbr{
\paragraph{Cost per iteration.}
The matrix $\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}$ is assembled once at $O(n^2(T+n_R))$ cost (the same setup as
the direct KKT solve) and cached; the largest eigenvalue $L$ is computed alongside it.  Each
subsequent iteration costs $O(n^2)$ for the matrix-vector product and $O(n\log n)$ for the
simplex projection, dominated by the $O(n^2)$ term.

\paragraph{Convergence.}
Since $\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}$ is SPD, the objective is $\lambda_{\min}(\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}})$-strongly
convex on $\Delta_n$.  Projected gradient descent with step $\tau \leq 1/L$ converges linearly:
\[
  \|w_k - w^\star\|^2
  \;\leq\;
  \left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa(\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}})}\right)^{\!k}
  \|w_0 - w^\star\|^2.
\]
The iteration count therefore scales as $O(\kappa(\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}))$, compared with
$O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ for CG.  On ill-conditioned problems (e.g.\ the unshrunk S\&P~500 system
with $\kappa \approx 10^5$) this gap is decisive.  Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage compresses
$\kappa(\Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}})$ monotonically as $\alpha$ increases and benefits both methods,
but it narrows the ratio of iteration counts only slowly.

\paragraph{Comparison with other solvers.}
The proximal solver occupies a niche between NNLS and the active-set methods.  Like NNLS it
handles the long-only and budget constraints in a single stage with no outer primal-dual loop.
Unlike NNLS, it does not form the normal equations at each step: NNLS incurs an implicit
$O(n^2)$ cost per Lawson-Hanson step \emph{and} its augmented matrix grows from $T \times n$ to
$(T+n)\times n$ when shrinkage is applied, paradoxically making NNLS slower with shrinkage.
The proximal solver's augmented matrix is fixed at $(T+n_R)\times n$ regardless of the
shrinkage path; on the S\&P~500 problem this gives $0.039$\,s with LW shrinkage versus
$0.11$\,s for NNLS.

At large $n$ the proximal method avoids the $O(n^3)$ factorisation of KKT direct.  The scaling
experiments (Figure~\ref{fig:loglog}) show it sitting between CG and KKT: at $n=2000$ the
proximal solver takes $0.35$\,s, versus $0.27$\,s for CG and $0.51$\,s for KKT direct.  CG
retains the edge throughout because its $O(\sqrt{\kappa})$ iteration count and $O(nT)$ per-step
cost (exploiting the matrix-free structure) compound favourably, whereas the proximal solver
pays $O(n^2)$ per step regardless of the matrix-free potential.

The proximal gradient approach is most attractive when the simplex-projection structure is
exploited further---for instance in the $\ell_1$-ball variant of the long-only constraint
studied in \cite{duchi2008}---or when one prefers a conceptually simple, loop-free
implementation at the cost of slower per-problem convergence.
}

\section{Ledoit-Wolf Shrinkage}
\label{sec:precond}

It is well known that the sample covariance matrix $\hat \Sigma = \frac{1}{T}X^\top X$ is a poor estimator
of the true covariance matrix $\Sigma$ when $n$ is ``not small" relative to $T$: its smallest
eigenvalues are severely downward-biased, and the condition number (the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalue) generally far exceeds that of the true covariance matrix.
Ledoit and Wolf \cite{ledoit2004} propose replacing the sample covariance matrix with a convex
combination of itself and the  scaled identity matrix (called the {\em shrinkage target}):
\[
\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}} = (1-\alpha)\cdot\hat\Sigma + \alpha\cdot \bar \lambda I \quad \mbox{with} \quad
\bar\lambda = \frac{\|X\|_{\text{F}}^2}{nT}~.
\]
Here $\|\cdot\|_{\text{F}}$ denotes the Frobenius norm of a square matrix, and
$\bar\lambda$ thus denotes the average eigenvalue of the sample covariance matrix.
The implementation  \tcr{Should we say "Our implementation"?}
uses a generalized $N\times N$ regularization shrinkage target matrix $R$
(satisfying $\mathrm{target} = R^\top R$), \tcr{The target cannot satisfy both target = $R$ and
target =  $R^\top R$}
so that
$\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}} = (1-\alpha)\cdot\hat\Sigma + \alpha \cdot R^\top R$.
\tcr{Maybe we need a more general notation here, to allow for a generalized shrinkage target.}
Classical Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage is recovered by setting $R = \sqrt{\bar\lambda}\,I$; for i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$
synthetic returns $\bar\lambda \approx 1$ so $R = I$ is equivalent.
The shrinkage intensity $\alpha \in (0,1)$ is
chosen analytically to minimize the expected Frobenius distance to the true covariance matrix, requiring no cross-validation. \tcr{This is not quite true. We are consistently estimating the optimal $\alpha$ only, we cannot guarantee to minimize the expected F-norm distance in finite samples.}
The effect on the spectrum is direct: the target term lifts every eigenvalue, bounding the smallest away from zero and
compressing the overall spread. \tcr{Not sure what is meant by ``lifts every eigenvalue". Classical LW shrinkage moves all sample eigenvalues towards their average. So large eigenvalues go down, whereas small eigenvalues go up.}
The condition number satisfies:
\[
\kappa(\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}}) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}(\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}})}{\lambda_{\min}(\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}})} \leq
\frac{(1-\alpha)\lambda_{\max}(\hat\Sigma) + \alpha\lambda_{\max}(R^\top R)}{\alpha\lambda_{\min}(R^\top R)}~,
\]
since $\lambda_{\min}(\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}}) \geq \alpha\lambda_{\min}(R^\top R)$ and, by Weyl's inequality applied to
$\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}} = (1-\alpha)\cdot \hat\Sigma + \alpha \cdot R^\top R$, $\lambda_{\max}(\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}}) \leq
(1-\alpha)\lambda_{\max}(\hat\Sigma) + \alpha\lambda_{\max}(R^\top R)$,
where $\lambda_{\max}(\hat\Sigma) = \lambda_{\max}(X^\top X/T)$ is
the largest sample eigenvalue. As $\alpha \to 1$, the bound approaches $\lambda_{\max}(R^\top R)/\lambda_{\min}(R^\top R)$,
which equals $1$ for the classical choice $R = \sqrt{\bar\lambda}\,I$, correctly reflecting that full shrinkage
yields a scalar multiple of the identity. For fixed data, the condition number decreases monotonically as $\alpha$
increases, with the rate governed by the spectral ratio $\lambda_{\max}(\hat\Sigma)/\lambda_{\min}(R^\top R)$. For CG applied to
$\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}}\,v = \mathbf{1}$, the convergence rate is governed by $\kappa(\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}})$; compressing the
spectrum directly reduces iteration counts. Subsequent work by the same authors provides an analytical nonlinear
shrinkage scheme that applies a different coefficient to each sample eigenvalue, typically achieving a smaller
Frobenius loss than linear shrinkage when $n/T$ is not negligible; see \cite{ledoit2020}.
Unfortunately, nonlinear shrinkage cannot be expressed as a simple augmented matrix
$\tilde{X}$, so incorporating it into the matrix-free framework would require a separate Krylov-compatible
preconditioner rather than a modified return matrix.

In the implementation, shrinkage is applied by augmenting the return matrix rather than forming $\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}}$
explicitly. Setting $c = 1 - \alpha$, the stacked matrix $\tilde{X}
= [\sqrt{c/T}\,X;\,\sqrt{\alpha}\,R]$ satisfies $\tilde{X}^\top \tilde{X} = \frac{c}{T}\,X^\top X + \alpha\,R^\top R
= c\,\hat\Sigma + \alpha\,R^\top R = \hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}}$,
so any of the solvers above can be applied to $\tilde{X}$ and produce exactly the LW-shrinkage portfolio weights.
For $R = \sqrt{\bar\lambda}\,I$, this results in the classical Ledoit-Wolf linear shrinkage: $\hat \Sigma_{\text{LW}} = (1-\alpha) \cdot \hat\Sigma + \alpha \cdot \bar\lambda\,I$.

From the perspective of numerical linear algebra, compressing the eigenvalue spectrum of the system matrix is precisely
what a preconditioner does. Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage therefore plays a dual role: it reduces statistical estimation error
and, as a side effect, accelerates iterative solvers by reducing the condition number of the input matrix relative the sample covariance matrix. The two motivations are independent
but arrive at the same modified matrix. \tcr{I do not quite understand the meaning of this last sentence.}

The proportional identity shrinkage target $\alpha \cdot \bar\lambda I$ is the simplest choice, but the preconditioning
literature suggests sharper alternatives. A factor-model preconditioner, replacing $\alpha \cdot \bar\lambda I$ with a
low-rank-plus-diagonal approximation $BB^\top + D$, would cluster eigenvalues more tightly at the cost of a slightly
more expensive application. Incomplete Cholesky factorization of the shrunk matrix provides a general-purpose option.
For rolling-window problems where the estimated covariance matrix changes slowly between periods, a preconditioner built from the
previous period's factorization could be reused with minimal update cost. None of these

require forming the covariance
matrix explicitly and each is compatible with the matrix-free framework described above.

\paragraph{Spectrum of $\hat\Sigma = \tfrac{1}{T}X^\top X$}
The eigenvalue structure of the sample covariance is qualitatively different from that of a random Gaussian matrix and
is the reason the S\&P~500 problem is a meaningful test case for the preconditioning argument. For a random $T \times n$
matrix with i.i.d.\ $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ entries, the Marchenko-Pastur law predicts that the bulk of the spectrum
lies in the interval $[\sigma^2(1-\sqrt{n/T})^2,\,\sigma^2(1+\sqrt{n/T})^2]$, giving a mild condition number that
grows modestly with $n/T$. The equity spectrum is nothing like this.

The largest eigenvalue is $\lambda_1 \approx 0.067$, corresponding the market factor, which alone accounts for $\approx 30\%$ of
total portfolio variance. To apply the Marchenko-Pastur law, we estimate $\sigma^2$ as the average squared entry of $X$,
\[
  \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{\|X\|_F^2}{nT} \approx 0.000457~,
\]
the natural entry-level variance of a daily percentage return. The MP upper edge $\hat\sigma^2(1+\sqrt{n/T})^2 \approx 0.00123$
then identifies 22 eigenvalues as statistically significant factors; the remaining 473 are consistent with noise yet
still span a wide range down to $\lambda_{\min} \approx 6 \times 10^{-7}$. The resulting condition number is
\[
  \kappa(\hat\Sigma) = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} \approx 109{,}000~,
\]
far larger than the $O(10)$--$O(100)$ range typical of random matrices at this aspect ratio.  The first ten
eigenvalues account for half the total variance, while the bottom half of the spectrum contributes less than $3\%$.
This extreme imbalance---a handful of dominant factors and a long tail of near-zero noise eigenvalues---is precisely
what makes CG slow without preconditioning (744 iterations) and what makes shrinkage decisive: lifting
$\lambda_{\min}$ by even a moderate $\alpha$ dramatically reduces the condition number and thus the iteration count to 82.

\section{Conclusion}

The long-only minimum variance portfolio problem, formulated
directly in terms of the returns matrix,
reduces to solving the $n\times n$ symmetric positive definite system $\hat \Sigma_{\mathrm{LW}}\, v = \mathbf{1}$ via
conjugate gradients, followed by a single rescaling $w = v/(\mathbf{1}^\top v)$.
The covariance matrix is never formed; all operations are matrix-vector products with $X$.

The empirical results on 495 S\&P~500 constituents over five years confirm the practical value of this approach.
For portfolios up to $n \approx 1000$, KKT direct is already 300--400$\times$ faster than CVXPY across both
shrinkage regimes and requires no convergence tolerance: it is the practical recommendation.  Without shrinkage, the
matrix-free advantage of CG is offset by high iteration counts (744 on S\&P~500 equity data) driven by the strong
factor structure of equity returns.  Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage \cite{ledoit2004} benefits both solvers: it compresses
the eigenvalue spectrum, reduces CG iterations to~82, and reduces active-set outer steps from~22 to~6, since a lower
condition number shrinks the unconstrained minimum-variance portfolio toward equal weights and fewer assets receive
negative weights in the primal step.  \tcr{That sentence is really a bit too long.}
At $n=495$ CG (0.0042\,s) edges KKT direct (0.0062\,s) by a factor of~1.5.
CG's advantage becomes decisive at large $n$: the scaling experiments show CG is roughly two times faster than KKT
at $n=2000$, a gap that widens even further as $n$ grows.

The shrinkage estimator of Ledoit and Wolf and the preconditioning theory of Golub and Van Loan \cite{golub2013} address
different problems but act on the same object, the eigenvalue spectrum of the system matrix. This connection, which the
two literatures do not usually make explicit, points toward richer preconditioners (factor-model or low-rank structured)
as a productive direction for future work.

The SPD structure extends naturally beyond the minimum variance setting. Adding a return term $\rho\hat \mu^\top w$ to the
